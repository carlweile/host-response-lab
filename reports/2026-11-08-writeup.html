Write-up · 8 November 2026 · public data, CPU only

How much of a published diagnostic AUC survives a hospital the model has never seen?

A small reproduction study on blood gene-expression data for the bacterial-versus-viral question. One command computes the optimistic number and the honest number from the same data and prints the gap between them.

Answer. Of a random cross-validation AUC of about 0.96, about 0.88 survives an unseen hospital on the raw scale and about 0.89 survives with a bedside-legal per-patient transform. Roughly 7 to 12 AUC points of the headline are hospital-specific. The drop is not a lucky split, not the chip family, not class weighting, and not a pipeline bug anyone has found. Its cause stays unattributed with five cohorts.

1. The question and why it matters

A patient arrives with a fever. Bacterial or viral? Antibiotics help one and not the other. Since about 2007 a line of research reads the patient's own immune response in blood, the genes switched on in white blood cells, and trains a classifier on it. Published areas under the ROC curve (AUC) sit at 0.93 to 0.95, and two such tests are FDA-cleared.

Those AUCs are almost always measured by splitting one study's patients at random. Every test patient then shares a hospital, a lab protocol and a microarray chip with the training patients. The model is free to learn those local quirks and is rewarded for it. A real test is used on a hospital it has never seen.

The gap between those two numbers is measurable from public data, is reported inconsistently in the literature, and in October 2026 we found no public code that computed it in one run. That gap is this project's whole subject. It is not a better classifier, not a new gene signature and not a clinical claim.

2. The design in plain words

Five public cohorts from the GEO archive, each from a different study and hospital, all with bacterial or viral labels, all on microarray chips.

Two models, an L2 logistic regression and a LightGBM gradient-boosted tree, both class-weighted, no gene selection anywhere, are trained and scored two ways on exactly the same matrix:

  • Random cross-validation. Patients of all five hospitals are shuffled and split five-fold. This is the number papers usually show.
  • Leave-one-cohort-out. Train on four hospitals, test on the fifth, repeat five times, average. This is the number a new hospital would see.

Three habits from wet-lab work are enforced in code. Labels are guilty until verified: the parser's class counts must equal the counts printed in each paper or the pipeline refuses to train. Rules before runs: every experiment's thresholds and verdict words are written into the lab book before the run, and the run does not get to change them (exceptions are disclosed in the lab book). Tests go red on broken code: each test constructs the bug it guards against and asserts the guard fires.

3. The four results

Result 1 · about 7 to 12 AUC points are hospital-specific

Both models look like a 0.95 test when the hospitals are mixed. On an unseen hospital the logistic model is a 0.88 test and the tree model a 0.83 test. The gap is positive in all 10 fold seeds and, read hospital by hospital, positive in all five for both models. The flexible model pays more, and it also carries its own randomness: over 10 model seeds LightGBM's held-out AUC moves between 0.780 and 0.861, so its gap reads as "clearly larger than logistic, size uncertain". The logistic model is deterministic.

Result 2 · a bedside-legal transform gives back 1 point (logistic) or 6 points (LightGBM)

Most published pipelines normalise a gene against the other patients of the same cohort. A bedside test sees one patient at a time, so that is not allowed here. Instead each patient's 12,040 genes are ranked against each other only; no other patient is read, in training or test. The gap does not vanish (third and fourth rows of the chart). For logistic it shrinks by about one point. For LightGBM it shrinks by about six, and that six survives LightGBM's own randomness: over 10 paired model seeds the per-patient run beat the raw run every time, mean gain +0.064, range +0.027 to +0.112, held-out 0.891 ± 0.006.

A whole-cohort rank normalisation would make the gap look closed. It was shown on synthetic data to be a transductive artefact, meaning it reads the test set, and its numbers are not quoted anywhere.

Result 3 · frozen published signatures have no random-CV bonus

If the gap comes from a model learning hospital quirks, a gene score that is not fitted on these data should show no gap. Two published scores were used exactly as printed in their papers and supplements: Herberg 2016 (two genes) and Sweeney 2016 (seven genes).

The frozen scores have no random-CV advantage; the pooled number is the same or lower because each cohort puts a fixed score on its own offset. Held out, they do about as well as or slightly better than the trained logistic model. This fits the reading that the Result-1 gap comes from fitting; it does not prove it. Limits: Herberg's gene FAM89A has no probe on two of the five chips, so it is scored on three cohorts, and Sweeney's own paper lists three of our five cohorts among those its genes were chosen on. One of the Sweeney authors confirmed our reading of their score by e-mail.

Result 4 · the drop is not the chip family, and class weighting is not where it lives

The held-out AUC differs by hospital. Does the size of the drop follow something we can name? Three candidates were pre-registered: chip family, age group, class mix. Before any of this ran, the new code had to reproduce all ten on-file held-out AUCs to within 0.005 (largest difference 0.0006).

  • Chip family: no. On all 20 ordered train-on-one / test-on-one cohort pairs (logistic, raw), same-family pairs transferred worse than cross-family pairs: mean AUC 0.760 vs 0.821, difference −0.061. With every training cohort cut to 30 arrays (20 random draws) the difference is −0.062, so it is not that the Illumina cohorts are small. Per-patient ranks −0.034; LightGBM −0.11 at each of three seeds.
  • Class weighting: not where the drop lives. Leave-one-cohort-out with weighting switched off moves the mean held-out AUC by +0.0005 (logistic) and +0.003 (LightGBM, 10 model seeds; per-seed range −0.029 to +0.032). A first 3-seed read gave +0.0195 and "undecided"; ten seeds showed that was seed luck.
  • Age: not testable from public sources. GSE63990 mixes ages 6 to 88, and neither GEO (0 of 280 arrays carry an age) nor the paper gives per-patient ages.

The sample-type row carries a limit and a side finding. The limit: GSE6269 is also the mostly pediatric, 80 %-bacterial, Affymetrix cohort, so with one PBMC cohort "PBMC" cannot be separated from "that one cohort". The side finding, LightGBM only: without GSE6269 in the training set the tree model becomes anti-predictive on the other young-children cohort, GSE40396 (held-out AUC 0.22 to 0.48 over three seeds, from 0.84 with it); the other three whole-blood cohorts barely move. Read it as training-set composition, not as sample type.

What remains: with five cohorts the drop is a pattern, not a cause. It stays unattributed. The batch split of the one cohort with two documented array batches has been run: a within-hospital batch costs AUC in one direction only, so it is a partial candidate, not a cause.

5. Honest limits

  • Flattered by exclusions. Co-infections and non-infectious inflammation are excluded, not modelled. Several published papers do the same; it makes everyone's numbers look better than an emergency department.
  • Five cohorts, 458 arrays. Roughly a tenth of the largest published comparison. Enough to measure a gap, not enough to name its cause.
  • Microarray only. Nothing here is checked on RNA-seq.
  • Labels are machine-parsed. Published counts match for all five cohorts, but only two of the five count checks were done by a human; the other three are AI-verified against the papers. No author of an original cohort has read the label table.
  • Which GSE63990 arrays are the re-runs is inferred, not proven; GEO has no patient ids.
  • Not a clinical failure rate. The FDA-cleared TriVerity test was validated prospectively in 1,222 patients at 22 sites (Nature Medicine 2025); its numbers are not comparable to these.
  • Per-patient ranking still needs a whole gene profile, which a 2- or 7-gene bedside assay does not have.

7. Reproduce it

python -m pip install -r requirements.txt
python run_pipeline.py --fetch        # the 5 GEO series into data/ (once, ~120 MB)
python run_pipeline.py --review       # label parse + published-count check: verified 5/5
python run_pipeline.py --train        # Result 1, both schemes (~1.5 min, CPU)
python run_pipeline.py --train --within-sample-normalize    # Result 2
python run_frozen_signatures.py       # Result 3
python run_transfer_matrix.py         # Result 4: chip family + 3-seed weighting
python run_transfer_matrix.py --balance-seeds 10            # Result 4: 10 LightGBM seeds
python tools/geo_age_check.py         # age fields in GEO
python run_whole_blood_check.py       # sample-type check
python run_batch_split.py             # GSE63990 batch split
python run_batch_deciding.py          # deciding batch test, 1000 draws
python -m pytest -q                   # 211 tests

No GPU is used anywhere. results/ is committed, so every number above can be diffed against a re-run, and each runner reports its own reproduction gate before writing anything new.

8. Prior art, and the one paragraph that justifies the repo

The field is mature and commercial: Inflammatix TriVerity (29 genes, FDA-cleared January 2025, prospective validation in Nature Medicine 2025), MeMed BV (3 proteins, FDA-cleared), the Bodkin et al. 2022 comparison of 28 signatures across 51 datasets, the Chawla et al. 2022 robustness benchmark, and the MetaIntegrator R package with leave-one-dataset-out built in. The method here is not new.

Bodkin et al. answers a different question. They fitted a fresh model inside each dataset separately and cross-validated within it, stating that "creating dataset-specific models overcomes batch effects since each signature is optimized in each dataset". Sound for their question, but it removes the batch effect by construction instead of measuring it, and the paper reports no discovery-versus-external drop. Well-resourced groups do cross-cohort validation in primary papers, but their pipelines are not public.

The contribution that survives scrutiny: a small, public, runnable implementation where one command computes the optimistic number and the cohort-held-out number from the same data, the label parsing checks itself against published counts, and every verdict was written down before the run.
